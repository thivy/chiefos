# Scientific Editorial

Create an intellectually rich editorial image that translates a complex subject into a museum-grade scientific composition.

## Scene

For a single concept, place one dominant [SCIENTIFIC_SUBJECT] within a spacious asymmetrical composition on a warm-ivory archival-paper background. For content with multiple distinct ideas, organize one coordinated study or specimen per idea within the same editorial system. Every subject should be specific, recognizable, and rendered with enough structural accuracy to reward close observation.

Present the subject through [CONCEPTUAL_LENS], using scientific illustration and information design to reveal a meaningful relationship, process, comparison, or transformation. The image should clarify how the idea works rather than merely decorate it with scientific motifs.

Surround the primary subject with a restrained [EVIDENCE_SYSTEM] of diagrams, measurements, annotations, samples, or process traces. Every supporting element must refer to something visible in the composition. Use thin connectors and clear spatial relationships so the viewer can follow the logic without explanatory prose.

## Visual Treatment

- Precise scientific illustration informed by botanical atlases, natural-history plates, field journals, cross sections, or architectural drafting
- One dominant visual anchor and one clear intellectual idea per composition
- Spacious asymmetrical editorial grid with strong alignment and generous negative space
- Warm ivory, cotton paper, parchment, or soft off-white as the primary ground
- Muted sage, olive, dusty teal, slate blue, terracotta, ochre, charcoal, and warm gray as supporting colors
- One purposeful accent color, limited to approximately five percent of the image
- Delicate black or warm-charcoal linework; never use thick strokes
- Fine construction lines, measurement marks, crosshairs, coordinates, dashed connectors, and numbered references
- Subtle tactile materials such as paper grain, tracing paper, ink, pencil, watercolor bloom, glass, or transparent overlays
- Diffuse daylight or museum-gallery lighting with soft natural shadows
- Depth created through layered information rather than dramatic perspective or glossy 3D effects
- Implied movement through growth, migration, circulation, feedback, emergence, expansion, or decay
- High information density at close range while preserving a calm, readable silhouette at a distance
- In multi-subject compositions, use one shared visual grammar while varying specimens, diagrams, and evidence

The scientific subject, conceptual lens, and evidence system must communicate one coherent argument. The composition should feel like a page from a future research institute's field atlas: rigorous, tactile, curious, and meticulously edited rather than ornamental.

## Placeholder Definitions

### [SCIENTIFIC_SUBJECT]

A specific natural form, material, artifact, mechanism, or system that can carry the central idea. Choose a subject with visible structure that supports close study, such as branching, layering, segmentation, flow, symmetry, growth, erosion, or assembly. Describe its form, material, orientation, scale, and placement. Show enough of the subject to make it identifiable.

Examples:

- A mature dandelion seed head shown in profile, with several seeds separating into the air
- A translucent cross section of branching coral centered slightly left of the page
- Three mineral crystals arranged from raw formation to fractured specimen
- A river delta viewed from above, with its channels spreading across the lower half of the composition

### [CONCEPTUAL_LENS]

The scientific or systems-based idea revealed through the subject. Use the subject's real structure or behavior to explain a process, relationship, comparison, or change over time. The visual interpretation should be conceptually grounded and immediately legible from the composition.

**Template**

Use [VISIBLE_FEATURE_OR_BEHAVIOR] to reveal [IDEA_OR_PROCESS], showing how [STAGE_OR_COMPONENT] connects to [STAGE_OR_COMPONENT].

Examples:

- Use the dandelion's dispersing seeds to reveal how one idea propagates through a network, from origin to independent adoption.
- Use the coral's branching cross section to compare biological growth with resilient decentralized infrastructure.
- Use the crystals' changing forms to show pressure, fracture, and reorganization across three stages.
- Use the delta's branching channels to map how information divides, recombines, and reaches multiple outcomes.

### [EVIDENCE_SYSTEM]

A small set of supporting visual elements that makes the conceptual lens credible and understandable. Select only evidence that directly explains the subject, such as a magnified detail, sequence, taxonomy, scale marker, field-note fragment, process arrow, material sample, coordinate, or comparison diagram. Arrange these elements as one connected system rather than scattered decoration.

**Template**

Connect [SUPPORTING_ELEMENT] to [VISIBLE_FEATURE] with [CONNECTOR_TYPE], and use [SECONDARY_ELEMENT] to clarify [RELATIONSHIP_OR_CHANGE].

Examples:

- Connect two circular magnifications to the seed's barbs and flight path with hairline leaders, then add a three-step dispersal sequence.
- Link numbered branch nodes to a compact resilience diagram using dashed paths and a small scale reference.
- Align material swatches beneath the crystal stages and annotate pressure changes with fine vertical measurements.
- Trace three delta channels with muted color and connect them to a small flow-ratio key and topographic contour study.

Use short, intentional labels only when the image model can render them clearly. Prefer numbers, symbols, specimen IDs, scale marks, and compact scientific notation over paragraphs. Never display placeholder names or prompt instructions in the image.

## Avoid

- Scientific decoration with no explanatory relationship to the subject
- Multiple competing focal points or disconnected collections of attractive specimens
- Generic corporate infographics, dashboards, presentation templates, or SaaS interface styling
- Decorative cards, rounded UI panels, glassmorphism, pill-shaped labels, or modern app iconography
- Perfect symmetry, rigid poster layouts, or evenly tiled content
- Thick outlines, heavy arrows, filled icons, cartoon graphics, clip art, or stock-vector styling
- Bright gradients, neon glow, cyberpunk effects, holograms, or excessive 3D rendering
- Dense annotation that overwhelms the primary idea or eliminates negative space
- Fake paragraphs, prominent illegible typography, arbitrary equations, or meaningless data
- Unrelated botanical, molecular, geometric, or archival motifs added only to appear scientific
- Heavy grunge, plastic surfaces, harsh reflections, HDR lighting, or strong drop shadows
- Unrequested logos, watermarks, captions, publication names, or decorative headlines
